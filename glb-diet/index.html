Section 02, the recipe

Five steps, measured on your model.

The whole pipeline runs on @gltf-transform/cli. No manual Blender re-export, no quality loss on the hero views.

  1. 01

    Dedup, prune, weld

    Remove duplicate accessors and unused data, merge coincident vertices. Free, no visual change.

    gltf-transform dedup in.glb mid.glb
  2. 02

    Simplify geometry, carefully

    Target 70 to 95 percent of original tris on dense meshes. Preserve silhouette by locking boundary edges.

    gltf-transform simplify mid.glb mid.glb --ratio 0.75 --error 0.001
  3. 03

    Compress meshes with meshopt

    Quantise and compress positions, normals and UVs. Decoded in the browser with the meshopt decoder.

    gltf-transform meshopt mid.glb mid.glb --level medium
  4. 04

    Textures to WebP, right-sized

    Cap maps at 2048, drop to 1024 for small on-screen area, convert PNG and JPEG to WebP.

    gltf-transform webp mid.glb out.glb --slots "*" --quality 80
  5. 05

    One command, same result

    In production I run the full chain so clients can reproduce it.

    gltf-transform optimize in.glb out.glb --compress meshopt --texture-compress webp --texture-size 1024
Section 03, checklist

What I check on your model before I touch it.

  • Scale and unitsmetres, with the pivot at the base. Avoid 100x models.
  • Pivotsorigin on the ground, child parts have local pivots for animation.
  • Normalsconsistent facing, no inverted shells. Smoothing groups match the design.
  • UVsno overlaps on unique maps, tiling only where it is meant to tile.
  • Texture sizespower-of-two, matched to on-screen pixels. 4k is almost never needed on the web.
  • Material countmerged where possible so draw calls drop.
  • Animation clipsnamed, trimmed, no empty tracks, in seconds.
  • Compression supportyour three.js loader has the right decoder, meshopt or Draco or KTX2.
